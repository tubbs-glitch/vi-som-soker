You cross the yard toward the house. The gravel is quieter than gravel ought to be, as if each stone has been set down on purpose. The kitchen window stands a finger ajar. Nothing comes through it — no singing, no static, no breath of air. Only the low even tone you have been hearing since the shed door. The platter is working. Nothing else.

In the hall the ceiling lamp clicks on, a filament that does not quite remember the work. It lights anyway. You stand with one hand on the doorframe and let the eyes get used to it.

The kitchen switch has been down so long you cannot remember it up. You do not go to it.

You move toward the living room. The record player stands where it stood. The smoked glass lid is lifted a finger's breadth. The platter turns at the same even speed — thirty-three you think, though you have never had the ear to tell the difference. The indicator on the front is lit orange.

The needle is not down.

It rests precisely where you remember — at the outer rim of the record, a hair above the groove. The arm points in toward the label without touching it. The record comes round, round, round. The groove passes under the needle and the groove goes on. Nothing catches.

You stand a moment. It is worse than quiet. A record turning without sound is a promise that is not being kept.

You think of Alice's note, the line you read and did not stop on. *LP starts at 22:47.* She meant the needle. She meant someone would lower it.

The clock in the hall is not the right clock for this. It runs slow. But somewhere in the house the time has gone forward all the same.
