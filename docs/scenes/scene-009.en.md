Before you go in you take the slip from your inner pocket. The number is written in an old man's precise hand — each digit too large, each digit still correct.

You stand on the step and dial. It rings five times. Six. Then someone picks up. No one says anything.

"Per-Magnus Berg?" you say.

"Yes." The voice is dry. As if it hasn't been used for a week.

"I'm Alice's niece. I'm outside her house now."

He breathes. It isn't silence. It is a quality of silence.

"The man at the petrol station said I should call." You say it to fill in. You say it also so he won't hang up.

"Yes," he says again. "He called me this morning. He said someone would come."

You stand with your back to the door. You look out toward the birches where Gunnar went.

"I don't know what to ask," you say.

He coughs once. A lung cough, not a throat cough. "Then you listen."

You listen.

"There's tar-stone in a jar in her kitchen. The shelf above the stove. The jar is labelled *torv-79*. It is not peat. It is tar-stone. It works only if you scatter it clockwise and start in the north. That's all I have to say about that."

He breathes once more.

"And one more thing. If you hear the hymn — the one with the fourth verse — then it is already late. There is not much you can do without the stone. There is barely something you can do with it either. But then you have something."

You stand quiet.

"I am thirty-seven years too late," he says. "Tell her that if she's still there to be told. Tell her from Per-Magnus. She'll know what it means."

You say you will.

He says nothing. It isn't silence. It is the receiver being set down.

You stand with the phone in your hand. You stand there until the battery indicator dims at the edge of your sight.
