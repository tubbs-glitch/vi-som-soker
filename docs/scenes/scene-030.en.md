You walk out from the dairy. You take nothing. The drawing stays in the chest because you already remember it.

The path out onto the bog begins at an old field fence that has fallen to the right. It is not a path any more — only a line of laid planks, leaning, half sunk, in places gone altogether. You go where it goes. You drop into the bog twice, once to the knee. The water is cold under the peat in spite of the June.

It is half past eleven at night, and the sun touches the mountain without going below it. The whole landscape is blue and faint, like a picture taken too early in the morning and forgotten in the developer.

The midges are a cloud. They land on your neck, in your ears, at the hairline. You do not wave them off. You stopped waving five hundred metres back.

You walk due northwest. The stand of birches lies where he said it would.

It is four birches. Three of them are standing. The fourth broke once, in the seventies, and grew out in an arch. Beneath it lies a stone.

It is not large. It is not a gravestone in the churchyard sense. A bog-stone — one of the grey limestone blocks that breach the hill ground — that someone has chiselled clean and turned so the flat side faces you. On the flat side, in clean stonework, in small letters, but deep:

*Bertil Lindblom*
*1935 — Midsummer 1979*

Nothing else. No verse. No flowers.

It does not stand straight. The frost has pushed one corner up.

You stand there. You stand there a while. You do not say anything because you have nothing to say. You think of a dog called Tora who lay beside him on a Friday and a Saturday. You think of your mother who has had a brother she never named. You think how the frost will go on pushing, and how in fifty years the stone will lean the other way.

The bog steams. It steams the wrong way against the wind, but there is no wind, so it does not matter. You stand with your trousers wet to the thigh.

On the way back you stumble on a loose plank. You do not fall. You think it is good that no one sees you.
