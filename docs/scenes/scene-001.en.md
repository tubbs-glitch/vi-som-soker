Wednesday night, and you've forgotten to eat. The flat smells of the coffee you made this morning and never finished. There is a tax letter open on the table, your work diary beside it, a row of small crosses through the Thursdays you no longer go in. The window is open. Down on Götgatan a bus brakes at Medborgarplatsen, and somewhere beneath the street the underground gives its faint moving rumble. The day's heat is still in the walls.

You fill a glass at the sink. You drink half of it. You set it down.

The phone rings.

It is not a number you have stored. You recognise the dialling code anyway — 0680, the long northern code — the way you recognise the smell of pine tar when you pass a barn. Some numbers you keep without meaning to.

You pick up.

'It's Gunnar Sandgren,' the voice says. 'I don't know if you remember me.'

You remember. A man's voice, worn by weather more than years. A face in a photograph, a hand holding a leash.

'It's about Alice,' he says. He says it flat, as if he has already said it twice to himself in the kitchen and now wants to be rid of it. 'She hasn't been seen for three days.'

You ask whether he's called the police.

'I wouldn't know what to tell them,' he says. 'She's a grown woman. She's at home. The car is on the drive. The post is in the box. It's just — she walks past my place on Wednesdays. She hasn't done it this week.'

He says nothing for a moment. You hear him take one breath in.

'I think you should come up,' he says. 'If you can manage it.'

You say you will. You say it before you have decided.

He thanks you. He says good night. Then the line is quiet.

You stand at the table with the receiver still at your ear a second after he is gone. On the diary, the page is open at June: fourteen days to Midsummer's Eve. The bus down on the street has already let off its passengers and gone. The glass on the draining board is still half full.
