You are inside. The door has closed behind you on its own. It is dark.

Not night-dark. Not the city dark with lamps outside the window. This is a dark that has settled indoors, and the blue dusk outside does not reach in. You stand still with your back to the door.

You hear a clock somewhere further in. It ticks slower than it should.

Something smells of pine tar. Something smells of old tea. Something smells of a dog long gone.

You set your hand to the wall to find the switch. You find it. You press. Nothing. You remember Gunnar said something about the storm.

You take one step in. The rug gives under your foot. You sense — sense more than see — a long narrow corridor. A coat on the left, tall as a person. The white shape of a hat on a shelf. On the opposite wall, things hanging in a row. You count them with your eyes alone: seven. Seven hooks. One is empty. You know it without being able to say how.

You stand with the bag in your hand. You have not set it down. There is nobody here to greet.

## In the torch's beam

You click the torch on. The beam is yellow and narrow — a cone three hand's-breadths wide, no more.

You move it slowly. It falls across the rug at your feet — blue and grey, worn through where feet have crossed it for forty years. You lift it. It falls across a coat on a hook. Green velvet, the elbows smooth. You know it without knowing how. It is Alice's.

You shift the beam to the right. It finds a blue enamel mug on a shelf. The letters *A.L.* in black ink. The white worn off the handle.

You turn it to the opposite wall. The beam moves along leather hanging in a row. Six strops with name plates. Seven hooks. The seventh empty. You don't read the names. You only move the beam on.

Behind you it is still dark. You know the clock is ticking from that side. You don't put the beam there yet.

Ahead: a glimpse of the doorway to the living room. Another, to the kitchen. A stair running up, the beam not reaching the top. Beside you a narrower door you remember leads down.

## When the power is on

You are inside. The door has closed behind you without your closing it — it swings back on its own, slow, weighted, as though the house draws breath when somebody arrives.

The hall is long and narrow. A rag rug in blue and grey, worn through where feet have crossed it for forty years. A coat hangs on a hook — Alice's, green velvet, the elbows smooth. A white sun-hat on the shelf. A blue enamel mug on the shelf too, the letters *A.L.* in black ink on its side, the white worn off the handle where a thumb has rested. Your aunt's name is Alice Lindblom. You don't know who else A.L. would be.

On the wall opposite the coat there are leashes. Seven of them. Six have leather strops with names written in ink — you don't read them yet, you only count. Seven hooks. The seventh is empty.

It is quiet. Not the quiet an empty house has, something else — as if someone has just walked out of a room before you came in. A clock ticks somewhere in the living room. You hear that it runs slow. You don't hear how you know.

The hall is lit now — a bare bulb in the ceiling shows a steady yellow that does not belong to the blue outside.

The hall smells of pine tar, old tea, a little of dog, a little of dog that is long ago.

You have not taken off your jacket. You have not set down your bag. There is nobody here to greet.

You take two steps in. The rug gives under your foot. From here you can see into the living room — a corner of a record player, a sofa-drawer pulled open, a folder half-spread on the low table. The kitchen door stands open the other way. The staircase runs ahead of you. Beside you, a narrower door you remember leads down to the cellar.

You stand a moment longer. Then you decide not to stand any longer.

## Back in the room

You are back in the hall. Seven leashes on the wall, one hook empty. To the right, the living room; to the left, the kitchen. The stairs run up ahead of you. The cellar door is beside you.
