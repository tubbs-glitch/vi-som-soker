You go out again. The door is heavier on the way out than on the way in, though it may be only your arm losing patience now. You pull it shut behind you and hear the click that is not a lock — only the door meeting its threshold.

It is some time after midnight. There is no telling where in the hours you are. The sun stands low above the ridge to the north-west, cold and blue and without direction. The shadows of the house fall two ways at once and no way at all. The light is the light of an early morning. It is not morning.

The wall of the house at your back is Falun red and crazed. The corner-boards have begun to peel. One slate is missing by the chimney — it has been missing some time, because the tin beneath has already weathered. The storm did not take that one.

In front of you: the yard. The garden opens to your right, toward the edge of the wood. The three rowans are where they have always been. You cannot see them clearly from here — a shrub has been left to grow up around them. To your left, set back, the woodshed in the same Falun red. It leans a little. The car is on the gravel where you left it. Beyond all of this, along a path that is not quite a path, you can make out a metal-clad building at the edge of your seeing — you had not thought of it before.

You stand on the lowest step. The midges find you in two seconds. You take four paces out onto the gravel.

From here you cannot see the line pole. It must stand down by the corner, hidden behind a hedge that has gone up, or behind the rowans themselves. You will have to walk to know.
