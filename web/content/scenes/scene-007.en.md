The road ends. It narrows through a last bend and then there is only the small yard in front of the house, the gravel matted with last year's rowan leaves.

You switch off the engine. You stay where you are.

It is twenty past nine in the evening. The sun stands over the line of the high country to the north-west — a hand's breadth above the silhouette. It will not go down. You hadn't been thinking about that and now you are.

The farmhouse is where it has always been. Falun red timber, white corners worn enough that bare paint shows under. Two windows on the ground floor, three above, a porch on the byre side. Behind the house, between the house and the mountain, the bog opens out — flat, glossy, impossible to cross in a straight line. Beyond the bog the bare uplands rise. They are six kilometres off and they look like one.

The car is on the drive. Alice's car. A green Saab 9-3 from the early two thousands. It is dusty but no more than a car not driven for a week ought to be. The back tyres sit a little into the gravel.

The postbox at the road is full. Mail sticks out. Two local papers, an envelope with a blue border.

You open the door. The gnats find you at once — at your hairline, at your wrists, around your ears. The smell off the bog is tarn-water and lilac and something sweet underneath.

You take a few steps from the car and you stop.

One of the upstairs windows stands ajar. Not open. Only ajar. The curtain does not move. There is no wind.
